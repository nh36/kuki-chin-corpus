This dossier is a broader research layer behind the current print-facing stem-alternation slice. It treats the autogenerated reports as a discovery layer only and checks candidate pairs against the aligned Bible corpus before accepting them as print evidence. Unless otherwise noted, the token counts below are exact-token counts from a direct scan of `data/verses_aligned.tsv`; derived forms such as `muhna`, `theihna`, `piakna`, `piangsak`, `ngaihsut`, `neihsak`, and similar expansions are discussed separately rather than folded into those exact counts.[^counts]

# Literature expectations

The secondary literature agrees on the basic point: Tedim has a two-form verbal system whose distribution is grammatically conditioned rather than lexically accidental [@henderson1965; @zamngaihcing2017]. Henderson calls the forms Form I and Form II and links them to the contrast between conclusive and inconclusive verbal phrases. Her description makes final conclusive predication the core home of Form I and inconclusive or adjunctive phrasing the core home of Form II [@henderson1965]. Zam Ngaih Cing uses the labels Stem 1 and Stem 2 and frames the same system more morphosyntactically, not only in terms of clause type but also in terms of environments such as negatives, nominalizations, and certain derived constructions [@zamngaihcing2017].

The two sources are therefore compatible at a broad level but not reducible to one mechanical rule. Henderson foregrounds finite clause type and mood-like contrasts, while Zam Ngaih Cing makes more explicit claims about negative clauses, nominalization, and benefactive or comitative derivation [@henderson1965; @zamngaihcing2017]. The safest expectation for the Bible corpus is therefore not "Form II always means subordinate" or "negative always triggers Form II", but rather that Form II should be especially visible in dependent, inconclusive, nominalized, or otherwise derived environments.

The literature also predicts that the formal alternation will not look uniform across all verbs. Henderson and Zam Ngaih Cing both describe several morphophonological pathways, including stem-final additions such as `+h`, `+k`, and `+t`, as well as broader substitution patterns [@henderson1965; @zamngaihcing2017]. That matters for the dossier because some Tedim Bible forms that look like possible Form II material are actually better explained as compounds, lexicalized derivatives, or semantically specialized items. The dossier therefore keeps a separate category for forms that are report-visible but unsafe as clean Form I / Form II evidence.

Several immediate cautions follow from this inventory. First, high exact counts do not automatically mean clean stem-alternation evidence. `thei ~ theih` has very high counts, but much of that material is constructional or nominalized. Second, low exact counts do not automatically mean "not a pair". `nusia ~ nusiat` looks real, but its Form II evidence is concentrated in dependent and irrealis contexts rather than in a single neat paired paradigm. Third, some report-visible candidates should not be treated as simple stem evidence at all. `honkhia ~ honkhiat` and `hu ~ huh` fall into that last category in the current corpus.

# Syntactic environment evidence

## Ordinary affirmative finite predicates

The clearest Form I evidence is still ordinary finite predication. `mu` in Genesis 1:4, `za` in Genesis 3:8, `ngai` in Genesis 4:23, `nei` in Genesis 11:30, and `nusia` in Genesis 2:24 are all straightforward finite uses:

- Genesis 1:4: `a mu hi` shows a bare finite Form I predicate.
- Genesis 3:8: `amaute in za uh` shows bare finite `za`.
- Genesis 4:23: `ka aw ngai un` gives an imperative/hear-type Form I use.
- Genesis 11:30: `ta nei lo hi` preserves finite `nei` even under negation.
- Genesis 2:24: `a nu le a pa nusia-in` gives a clear finite/serial Form I use.

This part of the evidence fits the literature well. Form I is easy to find in main predication and imperative or matrix clause uses.

## Negative contexts

The Bible evidence does **not** support a simple corpus rule that negative clauses automatically select Form II. Some negatives clearly stay with Form I:

- Genesis 2:17: `na ne kei ding hi`
- Genesis 11:30: `ta nei lo hi`
- Leviticus 26:21: `keima thu na ngaih kei lai uhleh`

At the same time, Form II can appear in negative or irrealis-heavy complexes:

- Joshua 24:16: `Topa nusiat ding ka ngaihsun ngei kei ding uh hi`
- Deuteronomy 28:20: `Topa na nusiat uh a`

The safest reading is that negative environments are only one part of the Form II story, not a sufficient diagnostic on their own. This is one place where the Bible corpus looks less tidy than a compressed literature summary might suggest.

## Nominalizations in `-na` and related forms

Nominalization is the strongest recurring environment for Form II in the current corpus. This part of the literature expectation is well supported.

- Genesis 19:19: `note' muhna-ah`
- Genesis 2:17: `a pha le a sia theihna singkung`
- Genesis 10:29: `Jobab' pianna pa`
- Genesis 4:5: `ama piakna`

These are exactly the kinds of contexts where the report layer is most useful if handled carefully. It often *finds* the right stem family, but it does not always distinguish a bare Form II verb from a nominalized Form II derivative. For the dossier, that distinction matters: `muhna` and `theihna` are strong evidence **for** the stem alternation, but they are not the same thing as bare finite Form II.

## Temporal clauses such as `... ni-in`

Genesis 2:17 remains the cleanest single verse for the dossier because it contrasts Form I and Form II in one sentence:

- `na ne kei ding hi`
- `tua na nek ni-in`

This directly supports the expectation that dependent temporal structure can favor Form II even where the main-clause predicate remains Form I. It is much stronger evidence than simply tagging a verse as "negative".

## Relative and attributive contexts

The Bible corpus supports Form II in some relative-like or attributive environments, but not uniformly.

- 2 Samuel 23:8: `David' neih mi thahatte`
- Genesis 38:1: `Hirah min nei mi`
- Exodus 36:8: `a hih thei mi`

This is an important mixed zone. `David' neih mi` is good evidence that Form II can surface in an attributive or possessed-nominal environment. But `nei mi` and `thei mi` show that not every relative-like noun phrase automatically selects Form II. The questionnaire's A/S and P-relative headings therefore need manual checking rather than blind reuse.

## Subordinate and clause-chaining contexts

Subordinate evidence is real, but it appears through several different constructions rather than one uniform subtype.

- Genesis 24:52: `a zak ciangin`
- Deuteronomy 2:14: `Kadeshbarnea i nusiat a kipan`
- Genesis 2:17: `na nek ni-in`

These examples broadly support the literature expectation that Form II is favored in dependent or clause-linking environments. But they also show that the corpus tends to express that dependence through `ciangin`, `a kipan`, purposive `nadingin`, and nominalized phrases, not just through a simple "subordinate clause" bucket.

## Causative and other derived environments

This is where the discovery layer is most misleading. Some report examples are not wrong because the pair does not exist; they are wrong because the verse is really showing a causative or otherwise derived form rather than a clean stem alternation.

- The `pia ~ piak` questionnaire opens with Genesis 1:1, Genesis 1:12, and Genesis 1:20, but those are `piangsak`, not `piak`.
- The `nei ~ neih` questionnaire includes `neihsak`, which is causative and should not be counted as direct Form II evidence.
- The `honkhia ~ honkhiat` family is heavily lexicalized as `honkhia` 'deliver' and does not behave like a clean paired showcase in the current corpus.

Derived material is still useful evidence for the wider morphology, but it should not be treated as if it were identical to bare Form II.

## Lexicalized compounds and report noise

Several high-frequency strings show why the autogenerated questionnaire cannot be accepted at face value.

- `ngaih` is swamped by `ngaihsun`, `ngaihsutna`, and related lexical families.
- `zak` overlaps with broader sensory or audience-type readings, not just ordinary hearing.
- `thei` and `theih` participate heavily in modal, purposive, and nominalized constructions.
- `piang` competes with `piangsak`, `pianna`, `pianzia`, and other derived family members.

The duplicate `thei/theih` sections in `05-verb-11-vsa-questionnaire.md`, with contradictory PSC labeling, are a clear warning sign. The dossier can still use the questionnaire to find candidate verses, but not to decide the analysis for them.

# Expansion beyond the current six-pair slice

The first print slice covered `mu ~ muh`, `ne ~ nek`, `nei ~ neih`, `thei ~ theih`, `piang ~ pian`, and `pia ~ piak`. The broader dossier changes the picture in two ways: it strengthens some omitted candidates, and it also identifies some report-visible pairs that should stay out of print for now.

| Pair | Dossier verdict | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `za ~ zak` | print grammar prose now, with caveat | Exact Form I and Form II tokens are both common, and `a zak ciangin` is a useful dependent-clause example, but example choice must avoid broader sensory and lexicalized readings. |
| `ngai ~ ngaih` | dossier-only evidence pending review | The pair is real enough to keep, but the lexical family is dominated by `ngaihsun/ngaihsut` material and the report glosses are unstable. |
| `nusia ~ nusiat` | dictionary entry now; grammar prose later | Exact Form II is real and constructionally promising, especially in dependent or irrealis contexts, but the slice still lacks a single clean reader-facing pair of examples. |
| `honkhia ~ honkhiat` | exclusion for now | `honkhia` is common, but the solitary `honkhiat` hit and the lexicalized/compound behavior make it unsafe as simple stem evidence. |
| `hu ~ huh` | exclusion for now | The apparent pair crosses lexical category and sense boundaries: `hu` is often noun-like 'breath', while `huh` is help/helper. |

Among the omitted pairs, `za ~ zak` is the strongest candidate for eventual inclusion in the print slice. `nusia ~ nusiat` is promising, but it is more likely to enter first through a carefully caveated dictionary entry than through a headline grammar example. `ngai ~ ngaih` should stay in the dossier until the lexical-family problem is sorted out.

# Evaluation of the current print slice

The slice was right to treat `mu ~ muh`, `ne ~ nek`, and `nei ~ neih` as draft-ready. Those are still the strongest pairs in the dossier, and none of their current examples now looks unsafe.

The slice was also right to keep `thei ~ theih` and `piang ~ pian` provisional. The dossier broadens the evidence for both, but it does not simplify it. `thei ~ theih` is still tangled with modal and purposive constructions, and `piang ~ pian` is still much easier to show through `pianna` and related derivatives than through a neat bare Form II paradigm.

`pia ~ piak` is somewhat stronger than the first slice made it look, because exact `piak` is common and Genesis 3:12 is a genuinely good manual example. Even so, it is reasonable to keep it caveated until the report layer is better insulated from `piangsak`, `piakna`, and similar lookalikes.

Among the omitted pairs, `za ~ zak` is the best candidate to add later. It has real exact-token support on both sides and a good dependent-clause example in Genesis 24:52. `ngai ~ ngaih`, by contrast, should stay out of the print slice for now, because the lexical-family problem is still too large. `nusia ~ nusiat` is promising, but it is better treated as dossier-backed expansion material than as an immediate new headline example.

No current pair needs to be removed. The existing examples remain safe, though two clarifications would help if the slice is hardened later: the `thei` example should remain explicitly caveated as constructionally mixed, and the `piang/pian` discussion should keep saying that the Form II side is most visible through nominalized or purposive forms rather than a bare finite verb.

# Interesting patterns and open questions

The clearest pattern in the current dossier is that Form II is especially visible through nominalization and dependent structure, not through a large inventory of neat bare finite pairs. `muhna`, `theihna`, `pianna`, and `David' neih mi` are not marginal accidents; they are the main place where the Bible corpus repeatedly aligns with the literature expectations.

The `+h`, `+k`, and `+t` groups do not behave equally clearly in the current corpus. The `+h` pairs are strongest when nominalized or attributive (`muh`, `neih`, `theih`). The `+k` pairs split: `ne ~ nek` is very strong, `za ~ zak` is usable but semantically broader, and `pia ~ piak` is obscured by derivational noise. The `+t` pairs split even more sharply: `nusia ~ nusiat` looks real, while `honkhia ~ honkhiat` does not yet behave like clean stem evidence.

Negative environments are less straightforward than the literature shorthand might suggest. The Bible corpus certainly allows Form II in negative-heavy and irrealis-heavy environments, but many negatives still surface with Form I. This may reflect construction type, translation style, or the interaction of negation with other operators such as `ding`, `ciangin`, and nominalization. At minimum, the dossier does not support using "negative" as a stand-alone diagnostic.

Causative and other derivational material repeatedly confuse the picture. `piangsak`, `neihsak`, `piakna`, and the lexicalized `honkhia` family all show that surface resemblance to a candidate stem is not enough. A likely next-stage editorial rule is that derivational morphology must be filtered before any future automatic stem-alternation ranking is trusted.

Bible translation style may also matter. The corpus overrepresents quotative, purposive, temporal, and formulaic subordinate structures. That is useful for exposing Form II, but it may also skew the evidence toward nominalized and dependent uses rather than the full range of spoken finite distribution. This remains a hypothesis rather than a conclusion.

# Corpus-wide analyzer audit

The new candidate TSV is still only a curated publication-review sample. Stem alternation needs more than that, because the underlying grammatical question is distributional: which Form I and Form II shapes are actually attested across the corpus, how often they occur, and which local environments favor them. A broader analyzer-based audit now exists in:

- `output/publication_review/stem_alternation_corpus_audit.tsv`
- `output/publication_review/stem_alternation_environment_summary.tsv`
- `output/publication_review/stem_alternation_pair_summary.tsv`

These files use the analyzer inventory plus the local token export to map attested stem-family tokens across the Tedim Bible. They are designed to answer where the analyzer sees Form I or Form II material, where the evidence is concentrated in nominalized, temporal, or clause-linking environments, and where apparent pairs are actually derivational, lexicalized, or otherwise noisy. The environment classifier is deliberately conservative. It is useful for corpus triage and packet revision, but it should not be mistaken for a full syntactic parser.

The next interpretive step should therefore start from these audit tables rather than from curated examples alone. The packet now has both a print-evidence candidate layer and a broader corpus-distribution layer; any later revision of the grammar or dictionary slice should use both.

# Recommendation

**B. The current print slice should stay narrow, but with a stronger evidence table.**

The dossier does not justify a wholesale rewrite of the slice, and it does not force the removal of any current pair. What it does justify is a stronger evidence table behind the slice:

- keep `mu ~ muh`, `ne ~ nek`, and `nei ~ neih` as the core print-ready set;
- keep `thei ~ theih`, `piang ~ pian`, and `pia ~ piak` explicitly caveated;
- add a small evidence table naming `za ~ zak` and `nusia ~ nusiat` as next-stage candidates rather than folding them immediately into the main prose;
- keep `ngai ~ ngaih` dossier-only for now;
- exclude `honkhia ~ honkhiat` and `hu ~ huh` from simple stem-alternation prose.

That approach preserves the current slice's caution while making the underlying evidence base much clearer.
