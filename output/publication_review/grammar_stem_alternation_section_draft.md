# Verb-stem alternation

## Overview of the Form I / Form II contrast

Tedim has a two-form verbal system. I use Henderson's Form I / Form II labels and treat Zam Ngaih Cing's Stem 1 / Stem 2 terminology as the same descriptive background, without turning this section into a literature review [@henderson1965; @zamngaihcing2017].

The main descriptive claim is narrower than the older slogans. Form I is especially clear in ordinary finite predication and many main-clause uses. Form II is especially visible in dependent, temporal, purposive, attributive, nominalized, and other constructionally bound environments. This draft therefore does **not** simply reduce Form II to subordination, negation, or nominalization, and it does **not** treat Form I as nothing more than a finite-clause form.

Throughout this draft, `output/publication_review/stem_alternation_citation_shortlist.tsv` is the quotation-safe layer. The syntactic-context matrix and pair-discussion plan organize the claims, but rows marked for review only remain descriptive evidence rather than printed quotations.

## Distribution by syntactic context

### 1. Finite and main-clause predication

Ordinary finite predication is where Form I is clearest. The most compact introductory example is `mu` in Genesis 1:4, and the same general profile recurs with Form I `pia`, `nusia`, `piang`, `zui`, `khial`, `kia`, and `sawlkhia` in ordinary clause-level predication. Even so, matrix Form II is not impossible: Genesis 3:12 has Form II `piak` in the same verse that also contains Form I `pia`, and Zechariah 6:15 preserves Form II `zuih` in a matrix conditional frame. The right claim is therefore that Form I is especially clear in main predication, not that Form II is categorically excluded there.

### 2. Imperatives and directives

Directive material reinforces the ordinary-clause affinity of Form I. The clearest quotation-safe rows are Form I `za` in 2 Samuel 20:16 and Form I `bia` in 1 Chronicles 16:29. At present, the quotation-safe directive rows do **not** include a comparably clean Form II imperative, so this subsection should support the broader distributional argument without treating directives as an independent diagnostic of the whole system.

### 3. Negative clauses

Negative clauses are relevant, but not mechanically decisive. Genesis 2:17 gives Form I `ne` in a prohibition, and Genesis 11:30 gives Form I `nei` in a negative possession predicate. Yet the same Genesis 2:17 verse also contains Form II `nek` in a temporal `ni-in` frame, which is exactly why the grammar should not simply reduce Form II to negation. Negative clauses matter because they intersect with several alternating pairs, not because they define a one-step trigger.

### 4. Dependent temporal clauses with `ciangin`

Dependent temporal clauses with `ciangin` are among the clearest places where Form II becomes visible as a constructionally bound form. The strongest quotation-safe examples are `muh` in Genesis 19:1 and `zak` in Genesis 24:52; further support comes from `theih` in 1 Chronicles 19:10, `pian` in 1 Corinthians 6:4, and `khialh` in 1 John 2:1. This context is therefore central to the system-wide argument, but it should still be presented as one important dependent frame rather than as the whole meaning of Form II.

### 5. Temporal `ni-in` contexts

The `ni-in` construction confirms that Form II is not confined to one temporal pattern. Genesis 2:17 gives the clearest quotation-safe example with Form II `nek` in a temporal clause. The corpus is much smaller here than in the `ciangin` set, so the subsection should stay compact, but it is important because it keeps the dependent argument from resting on a single frame.

### 6. Clause-linking with `kipan`

Clause-linking `kipan` frames are especially important for promoted-but-caveated pairs whose Form II evidence is structurally real but not quotation-ready. The key example is `nusia ~ nusiat`: the best exact Form II evidence appears in a clause-linking `kipan` environment, not as a clean finite contrast, and the relevant row is still tagged in a way that makes it unsuitable for direct quotation. This context therefore belongs in the grammar as a structural explanation of why some real Form II evidence is constructionally bound.

### 7. Purposive `ding / nadingin`

Purposive syntax is another major environment for Form II. The safest quotation-ready examples are Form II `biak` in 1 Chronicles 21:23 and Form II `piak` in 1 Chronicles 21:22. These rows show that purposive or irrealis-heavy syntax can make Form II prominent even when the same lexical pair is not best introduced through a neat finite contrast. The subsection should also point forward to `thei ~ theih`, whose best Form II evidence clusters in dependent and purposive syntax.

### 8. Nominalized `-na`

Nominalized `-na` forms are an important domain for Form II, but the grammar has to treat them carefully. The current shortlist does not provide quotation-safe rows for this subsection, so the discussion should stay structural: nominalization is one place where Form II is often robustly visible, but lexicalized nouns and compounds cannot simply be recycled as proof of verbal alternation. This is exactly where the filter against `biakna`-type and other lexicalized material has to be kept explicit.

### 9. Attributive / relative / possessed contexts

Attributive and relative material belongs with the broader set of non-final environments, but the strongest quotation-safe evidence currently comes from possessed or genitive attributive structure rather than from a clean `mi` relative clause. The clearest example is Form II `neih` in 2 Samuel 23:8, where the form appears in an attributive or possessed nominal construction. Relative `mi` contexts should still be discussed, but in this first draft they are better treated as part of the wider non-final distribution than as a separate showcase rule.

### 10. Modal and ability uses, especially `thei ~ theih`

`thei ~ theih` is the clearest reminder that some alternating pairs have to be described by construction type rather than by a simple lexical paradigm. The quotation-safe Form I anchor is `thei` in Genesis 2:16, where the verb appears in a modal or ability frame. The best Form II evidence is again constructionally bound, especially in dependent temporal syntax such as `theih` in 1 Chronicles 19:10, with further purposive evidence remaining descriptive rather than quotation-ready. This pair should therefore sit at the overlap of modal semantics and dependent syntax.

### 11. Quotative and say-complement contexts

Quotative and say-complement contexts matter, but they are also where functional material can swamp the lexical-verb story. The current shortlist does not provide quotation-safe rows here, and the context matrix shows why: verbs such as `ci ~ cih` dominate the environment, while the lexical alternation evidence is more mixed. The subsection should therefore be short and should mainly explain why quotative complements do not by themselves define the stem system.

### 12. Derived, causative, compound, lexicalized, and review-bucket material as filters

The grammar needs an explicit filter section. Derived `-sak` material, compounds, lexicalized families, and analyzer-review rows are not positive evidence contexts; they are the places where overgeneration has to be trimmed away. This is where rows involving `piangsak`, `zuihsak`, `khialsak`, `biakinn`, and `ngaihsutna` belong. They matter because they explain why some apparent pairs remain caveated or blocked, not because they should be promoted as ordinary Form I / Form II contrasts.

## Core showcase examples

The core table stays deliberately narrow. It is meant to introduce the system with the three pairs that give the clearest contrast between ordinary Form I uses and constructionally bound Form II uses.

| `ne ~ nek` | `ne` — Genesis 2:17 (negative prohibition on eating) | `nek` — Genesis 2:17 (dependent temporal `nek ni-in` clause) | A compact same-verse contrast: Form I appears in the negative prohibition, while Form II appears in a temporal non-final clause within the same verse. |
| `mu ~ muh` | `mu` — Genesis 1:4 (main finite perception predicate) | `muh` — Genesis 19:1 (dependent temporal `muh ciangin` clause) | The pair shows the basic finite-versus-dependent contrast without requiring a slogan that Form II is always subordinate. |
| `nei ~ neih` | `nei` — Genesis 11:30 (negative possession predicate) | `neih` — 2 Samuel 23:8 (possessed or attributive `David neih ...` construction) | The pair shows that Form II is also strong in attributive and possessed nominal structure, not only in clause chaining. |

## Pair-by-pair notes for promoted and difficult pairs

The promoted-pair inventory above is the quick reference layer. The difficult pairs below stay in the grammar because they expose the edges of the system, but they should not be overpromoted into the inventory table.

- `ngai ~ ngaih` has clean exact verbal rows and should remain in the discussion, but it is heavily contaminated by the `ngaihsun / ngaihsut / ngaihsutna` family. The pair therefore belongs in a difficult-case note rather than in the promoted inventory.
- `pua ~ puak` remains grammatically important because both sides have clean verbal evidence, but the shared base with `pua ~ puah` and the gloss mismatch still make the lexical identity unstable. It should be used to explain why exact Bible evidence does not by itself solve homophone and family overlap problems.
- `pai ~ paih` has very strong Form I evidence, yet the Form II side is thin and competes directly with the same-form questionnaire control `pai ~ pai`. This pair should stay visible as a real but difficult case.
- `tua ~ tuah` should remain in the grammar because the Form II side is verbally real, but the Form I side is still category-mixed and often non-verbal. It is therefore a cautionary difficult pair rather than a promotable contrast.
- `tua ~ tuak` is even more cautionary: the row matters because it shows how analyzer overgeneration can imitate a stem pair, while the Form I base remains overwhelmingly determiner-like and the Form II side is unstable. It belongs in the difficult tier precisely because it borders on rejection without yet being erased from view.
