\section{Deixis, pronouns, and nominal
domain}\label{deixis-pronouns-and-nominal-domain}

\subsection{Demonstratives / deixis}\label{demonstratives-deixis}

\emph{Source slice:
\texttt{output/publication\_review/grammar\_demonstratives\_print\_slice.md}}

\subsubsection{Scope}\label{scope}

This account offers a short treatment of Tedim demonstratives and
deixis. It is intentionally narrow. It focuses on a small set of
manually checked Bible examples and on the interaction between the core
demonstratives \texttt{hih} and \texttt{tua}, their plural forms, and a
few strong constructional extensions. It does not attempt a full
treatment of copulas, sentence-final particles, directionals, or broad
discourse structure.

\subsubsection{Overview of the demonstrative
system}\label{overview-of-the-demonstrative-system}

The current Bible corpus and the literature agree on a compact
demonstrative core: proximal \texttt{hih} and distal \texttt{tua}
\citep{henderson1965, zamngaihcing2017}. In Bible usage, however,
\texttt{tua} is not merely a spatial ``that''. It is also a strongly
anaphoric and discourse-linking form, especially in narrative and
temporal sequencing. The most economical description is therefore a
system with \texttt{hih} as the core proximal demonstrative and
\texttt{tua} as the core distal/anaphoric demonstrative.

The plural forms \texttt{hihte} and \texttt{tuate} behave transparently
as DEM + \texttt{-te}, and the Bible also gives strong constructional
material such as \texttt{hih\ bangin}, \texttt{tua\ bangin},
\texttt{tua\ ciangin}, and \texttt{tua\ ahih\ ciangin}. One caution
remains explicit throughout this slice: Zam Ngaih Cing gives distal
\texttt{huā}, while the Bible corpus overwhelmingly uses \texttt{tua} as
the ordinary distal/anaphoric form \citep{zamngaihcing2017}. The
relation is plausible, but it is not resolved here as a settled
orthographic identity.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Core demonstratives: \texttt{hih} and
\texttt{tua}}{Core demonstratives: hih and tua}}\label{core-demonstratives-hih-and-tua}

\texttt{Hih} is the clearest proximal form in the present corpus. It
works both as an identificational demonstrative and as an adnominal
determiner.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:dem-hih}{Genesis 5:1}
\reviewobjectline{Hih pen Adam’ suanlekhakte’ laibu ahi hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
hih & pen \\
\textsc{prox} & \textsc{top} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"This is the book of the generations of Adam."}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 5:1 is especially useful because it shows \texttt{hih} as the
unmarked proximal form in identificational prose. Genesis 9:12
\texttt{Hih\ pen\ ...\ thuciamna\ lim\ ahi\ hi} confirms the same
pattern, while Exodus 32:9 \texttt{Hih\ mite\ ka\ mu\ zo\ hi} shows
\texttt{hih} before a noun phrase in ordinary adnominal use.

\texttt{Tua} belongs to the same system, but its range in the Bible is
broader. It clearly marks distal or previously activated referents, and
it also extends naturally into anaphoric and discourse-linking uses.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:dem-tua}{Genesis 1:6}
\reviewwarninginline{[REVIEW PREVIEW WARNING: segmentation/gloss token mismatch in ex:dem-tua; showing padded alignment.]}
\reviewobjectline{Pasian in, “Tuite’ laizangah van kuumpi om hen la, tua van kuumpi in tui le tui kikhensak hen,” ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
tua & van & kuumpi \\
\textsc{dist} & firmament &  \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And God said, Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters."}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 1:6 is a clean adnominal example. Genesis 21:27
\texttt{tua\ mi\ nihte} is equally good for a more discourse-linked noun
phrase. The important descriptive point is that the corpus keeps using
\texttt{tua} where English may translate ``that'', ``the same'', or
simply a contextually given referent.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Plural forms: \texttt{hihte} and
\texttt{tuate}}{Plural forms: hihte and tuate}}\label{plural-forms-hihte-and-tuate}

The plural demonstratives are straightforward. The Bible corpus strongly
supports \texttt{hihte} and \texttt{tuate} as transparent plural
extensions of the singular forms.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:dem-hihte}{Genesis 48:8}
\reviewobjectline{Israel in Josef’ tapate a muh ciangin amah in, “Hihte kua ahi hiam?” a ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
hihte \\
\textsc{prox}-\textsc{pl} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And Israel beheld Joseph's sons, and said, Who are these?"}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:dem-tuate}{Genesis 7:20}
\reviewobjectline{Mual liante tungah tui omto lai a, tuate tungah pi sawmnih le nih thuk-in tuumcip hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
tuate & tungah \\
\textsc{dist}-\textsc{pl} & on-\textsc{loc} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"Fifteen cubits upward did the waters prevail; and the mountains were covered."}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 10:20 \texttt{Hihte\ pen\ ...\ Ham’\ suanlekhakte\ ahi\ hi}
shows the proximal plural in topic-like prose, while Genesis 2:19
\texttt{mipa\ in\ tuate\ bang\ a\ ci\ hiam} shows the distal plural as
an ordinary pronoun. Nothing in the present corpus suggests that these
need any more complex treatment than DEM + \texttt{-te}.

\subsubsection{Adnominal and pronominal
uses}\label{adnominal-and-pronominal-uses}

The same forms serve both as adnominal modifiers and as independent
pronouns. Adnominal use is easy to see in Exodus 32:9
\texttt{Hih\ mite}, Genesis 1:6 \texttt{tua\ van\ kuumpi}, and Genesis
21:27 \texttt{tua\ mi\ nihte}. Pronominal use is equally clear in
Genesis 5:1 \texttt{Hih\ pen}, Genesis 48:8
\texttt{Hihte\ kua\ ahi\ hiam?}, and Genesis 2:19
\texttt{tuate\ bang\ a\ ci\ hiam}.

The Bible also shows productive matter/topic phrases such as
\texttt{hih\ thu} and \texttt{tua\ thu}. In Genesis 24:9
\texttt{hih\ thu\ tawh\ kisai-in} points to the present matter under
discussion, while Genesis 6:6
\texttt{tua\ thu\ in\ ama\ lungsim\ dahsak\ hi} and Exodus 2:15
\texttt{Faro\ in\ tua\ thu\ a\ zak\ ciangin} show anaphoric reference to
something just reported. These are important for the grammar chapter,
even if they do not have to be first-round dictionary headwords.

\subsubsection{Discourse and temporal
deixis}\label{discourse-and-temporal-deixis}

This is where \texttt{tua} most clearly exceeds a merely spatial
description. In Bible narrative, \texttt{tua} is a major discourse and
temporal linker.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:dem-tua-ciangin}{Genesis 1:3}
\reviewobjectline{Pasian in, “Khuavak om hen,” ci hi; tua ciangin khuavak om pah hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
tua & ciangin \\
\textsc{dist} & time-\textsc{cvb} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And God said, Let there be light: and there was light."}
\end{reviewexample}

\texttt{Tua\ ciangin} works naturally as ``then / at that time'' in
event sequencing. Genesis 5:5 repeats the same pattern in obituary-style
narrative: \texttt{tua\ ciangin\ amah\ si\ hi}.

\texttt{Tua\ ahih\ ciangin} is even more explicitly a discourse bridge,
linking a prior state or event to the next clause.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:dem-tua-ahih-ciangin}{Genesis 2:21}
\reviewobjectline{Tua ahih ciangin Topa Pasian in mipa ihmut suak mahmah sak a, ama ihmut kalin a nakguhte khat la-in tua mun pen satak tawh a dimsak hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
tua & ahih & ciangin \\
\textsc{dist} & be.\textsc{3sg} & then-\textsc{cvb} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam, and he slept: and he took one of his ribs, and closed up the flesh instead thereof."}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 1:21 and Genesis 2:19 confirm that \texttt{tua\ ahih\ ciangin}
is one of the strongest discourse-transition frames in the present
corpus. It is better understood as a fixed demonstrative-temporal
construction than as a purely compositional spatial phrase.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Manner constructions with
\texttt{bangin}}{Manner constructions with bangin}}\label{manner-constructions-with-bangin}

The Bible also gives strong manner and discourse constructions built
from the demonstratives plus \texttt{bangin}.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:dem-hih-bangin}{Genesis 32:4}
\reviewobjectline{amaute hilhin, “Note in ka topa Esau kiangah hih bangin na ci ding uh hi: Na nasempa Jakob in, ‘Laban’ kiangah peemta-in tu ciang dong ka om hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
hih & bangin \\
\textsc{prox} & like-\textsc{cvb} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And he commanded them, saying, Thus shall ye speak unto my lord Esau; Thy servant Jacob saith thus, I have sojourned with Laban, and stayed there until now:"}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:dem-tua-bangin}{Exodus 14:30}
\reviewobjectline{Topa in tua bangin Egypt mite’ khutsung panin tua ni-in Israel-te honkhia a, tuipi gei-ah Egypt mi a site, Israel mite in mu uh hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
tua & bangin \\
\textsc{dist} & like-\textsc{cvb} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"Thus the LORD saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians; and Israel saw the Egyptians dead upon the sea shore."}
\end{reviewexample}

\texttt{Hih\ bangin} is especially good for direct instruction or quoted
manner, as also seen in Genesis 42:18 \texttt{hih\ bangin\ gamta\ un}.
\texttt{Tua\ bangin} often resumes or summarizes an already described
event, as in Genesis 50:21
\texttt{Amah\ in\ tua\ bangin\ ...\ amaute\ a\ hehnem\ hi}. These
patterns belong naturally in a demonstratives slice because the deictic
base still contributes to how the construction organizes discourse.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Deferred forms: \texttt{hi} and
\texttt{hih\ ciangin}}{Deferred forms: hi and hih ciangin}}\label{deferred-forms-hi-and-hih-ciangin}

\texttt{Hi} should be deferred. Exact-token \texttt{hi} is far too
entangled with copular, auxiliary, and sentence-final material to serve
as a safe demonstrative headword in this packet. Genesis 48:18
\texttt{Hi\ lo\ hi,\ pa\ aw;\ hih\ pen\ a\ suak\ masa\ ahi\ hi} shows
how quickly the shorter form is absorbed into other clause types. The
right place for \texttt{hi} is later work on copula or sentence-final
particles, not this first demonstratives packet.

\texttt{Hih\ ciangin} should also be deferred. The current dossier
showed that many apparent hits involve verbal \texttt{hih} ``do/be
thus'' rather than demonstrative \texttt{hih}. Genesis 18:10, the old
generated-report example, does not contain exact demonstrative
\texttt{hih\ ciangin}. For the present slice, \texttt{tua\ ciangin} is
strong enough to represent the temporal side of the system on its own.

\subsubsection{Editorial summary}\label{editorial-summary}

The current evidence supports a compact but stable demonstrative system.
\texttt{Hih} is the core proximal demonstrative. \texttt{Tua} is the
core distal/anaphoric demonstrative and should not be reduced to a
merely spatial ``that''. \texttt{Hihte} and \texttt{tuate} are
transparent plural forms, and the constructional extensions
\texttt{hih\ bangin}, \texttt{tua\ bangin}, \texttt{tua\ ciangin}, and
\texttt{tua\ ahih\ ciangin} are all well supported by manually checked
Bible examples.

Just as important, the chapter has to preserve its cautions. \texttt{Hi}
is not yet safe as a demonstrative headword, \texttt{hih\ ciangin} is
not yet a stable temporal counterpart to \texttt{tua\ ciangin}, and the
relation between Bible \texttt{tua} and the literature's \texttt{huā} is
plausible but unresolved. With those limits kept explicit,
demonstratives and deixis make a good narrow chapter for the next Tedim
packet.

\subsection{Pronouns / clusivity}\label{pronouns-clusivity}

\emph{Source slice:
\texttt{output/publication\_review/grammar\_pronouns\_print\_slice.md}}

\subsubsection{Scope}\label{scope-1}

This account presents a short print-facing chapter on Tedim personal
pronouns and closely related pronominal marking. It covers independent
personal pronouns, first-person plural forms and clusivity, possessive
prefixes, emphatic forms in \texttt{-mah}, reflexive and reciprocal
marking with \texttt{ki-}, and a cautious first treatment of
\texttt{hong-} and \texttt{kong-}. It does not attempt a full account of
demonstratives, interrogatives, quantifiers, TAM, or the wider verbal
agreement system.

\subsubsection{Personal pronouns}\label{personal-pronouns}

Earlier descriptions agree on a basic personal-pronoun system with
distinct first, second, and third persons and with plural forms for at
least the first and second persons
\citetext{\citealp[32-33]{henderson1965}; \citealp[sec.~3.2.1]{zamngaihcing2017}}.
Henderson explicitly lists \texttt{kei}, \texttt{nang}, \texttt{amah},
\texttt{ei/eite}, \texttt{ko/kote}, and \texttt{no/note}, while Zam
Ngaih Cing confirms the broader person-and-number system and the absence
of gender in the third person
\citetext{\citealp[32]{henderson1965}; \citealp[sec.~3.2.1]{zamngaihcing2017}}.
For print purposes, the following paradigm is stable enough to use:

{\def\LTcaptype{none} % do not increment counter
\begin{longtable}[]{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule\noalign{}
Person & Singular & Plural \\
\midrule\noalign{}
\endhead
\bottomrule\noalign{}
\endlastfoot
1 & \texttt{kei} & \texttt{eite}, \texttt{kote} \\
2 & \texttt{nang} & \texttt{note} \\
3 & \texttt{amah} & \texttt{amaute} \\
\end{longtable}
}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:pro-amah}{}
\reviewobjectline{Mipa in a zi' min pen Eve, ci hi. Bang hang hiam cih leh amah pen mihing khempeuh' nu ahi hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
amah \\
\textsc{3sg}.\textsc{pro} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'And Adam called his wife's name Eve; because she was the mother of all living.'}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:pro-note}{}
\reviewobjectline{En un, note le note' khit a na suanlekhakte uh le,}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
note \\
\textsc{2pl}.\textsc{pro} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'And I, behold, I establish my covenant with you, and with your seed after you;'}
\end{reviewexample}

These examples are ordinary independent pronouns rather than bound
agreement markers. \texttt{amah} functions as a free third-person form,
while \texttt{note} shows the equally straightforward second-person
plural. The printed chapter can therefore begin from free pronouns and
only then move to the more tightly bound prefixal system.

\subsubsection{First-person plural forms and
clusivity}\label{first-person-plural-forms-and-clusivity}

The main editorial problem in this slice is not whether Tedim has
clusivity, but how far the current evidence supports global labels for
the two first-person plural series. Henderson clearly distinguishes
\texttt{ei/eite} from \texttt{ko/kote} through pronominal-concord
prefixes, pairing the former with \texttt{i-} and the latter with
\texttt{ka-} \citep[32-33]{henderson1965}. Zam Ngaih Cing likewise
treats clusivity as a real feature of Tedim person marking, even though
her presentation of the plural forms is not identical in surface detail
\citetext{\citealp[sec.~3.2.1]{zamngaihcing2017}; \citealp[sec.~3.2.2]{zamngaihcing2017}}.
The separate clusivity dossier for this slice shows that sampled Bible
dialogue contexts strongly support \texttt{ko/kote} as exclusive, but
that \texttt{ei/eite} has both clear inclusive uses and less
straightforward uses in the current Bible evidence. Comparative Sukte is
useful here mainly as a contrast, since Singh does not describe the same
inclusive/exclusive opposition for Sukte
\citep[sec.~4.6.1]{sukte_grammar}.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:pro-eite}{}
\reviewobjectline{Tua ciangin Abram in Lot' kiangah, "Nang le kei' kikal, nang' gancingte le kei' gancingte' kikalah kitotna omsak kei ni. Bang hang hiam cih leh eite beh khat ihi hi."}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
eite \\
\textsc{1pl}.\textsc{pro} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'And Abram said unto Lot, Let there be no strife, I pray thee, between me and thee, and between my herdmen and thy herdmen; for we be brethren.'}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:pro-kote}{}
\reviewobjectline{Kote tawh kitenna hong bawl un. Na tanute uh kote' tungah hong pia unla, no a dingin ka tanute uh la un.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
kote \\
\textsc{1pl}.\textsc{pro} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'And make ye marriages with us, and give your daughters unto us, and take our daughters unto you.'}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 13:8 shows a genuinely inclusive use of \texttt{eite}, since
Abram explicitly includes Lot in the relevant group. It does not prove
that every \texttt{eite} token is inclusive. Genesis 34:9, by contrast,
is a strong diagnostic for exclusive \texttt{kote}, since Hamor
addresses Jacob's family from a distinct in-group. The present slice
therefore treats \texttt{kote} as exclusive and leaves the exact global
status of \texttt{eite} under review.\footnote{The earlier report-level
  reversal \texttt{kote} inclusive versus \texttt{eite} exclusive has
  now been removed. The safer editorial position is narrower:
  \texttt{ko/kote} can already be treated as exclusive, but
  \texttt{ei/eite} still shows mixed Bible-corpus behavior and should
  remain under review rather than receiving a single global label.}

\subsubsection{Possessive prefixes}\label{possessive-prefixes}

The same person-marking forms that appear in pronominal concord also
appear before nouns as possessive prefixes. Henderson treats
\texttt{ka-}, \texttt{na-}, \texttt{a-}, and \texttt{i-} primarily as
pronominal concord prefixes, while Zam Ngaih Cing foregrounds their
possessive use in noun phrases
\citetext{\citealp[32-33]{henderson1965}; \citealp[sec.~3.2.2]{zamngaihcing2017}; \citealp[sec.~3.3.4.1.1]{zamngaihcing2017}}.
For a printed grammar, the safest description is that the forms are
shared across possessive and agreement environments, but are easiest to
present first in their nominal use.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:poss-na}{}
\reviewobjectline{Kua' tanu na hi hiam, hong gen in. Na pa' inn-ah kote' giah nading a awng ding hiam? a ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
na & pa' & inn-ah \\
\textsc{2sg}.\textsc{poss} & father & house-\textsc{loc} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'Whose daughter art thou? tell me, I pray thee: is there room in thy father's house for us to lodge in?'}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:poss-a}{}
\reviewobjectline{Mipa in a zi' min pen Eve, ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
a & zi' \\
\textsc{3sg}.\textsc{poss} & wife \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'And the man called his wife's name Eve.'}
\end{reviewexample}

The same pattern is visible in \texttt{ka\ pa\textquotesingle{}\ inn}
`my father's house' and in the \texttt{i-} prefix that Henderson
associates with the \texttt{ei/eite} series
\citep[32-33]{henderson1965}. Singh's Sukte comparison is also helpful
here, since it shows cognate person-marking prefixes in possessive use
even though the wider systems are not identical
\citep[sec.~4.5.4]{sukte_grammar}. Plural verbal markers such as
\texttt{-uh} belong to the later agreement chapter rather than to the
pronoun inventory itself.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Emphatic pronouns in
\texttt{-mah}}{Emphatic pronouns in -mah}}\label{emphatic-pronouns-in--mah}

Emphatic pronouns are formed by adding \texttt{-mah} to a
personal-pronoun base
\citetext{\citealp[32]{henderson1965}; \citealp[sec.~3.2.6]{zamngaihcing2017}}.
In print, it is best to treat these forms neither as an unrelated
lexical series nor as mere stylistic variants, but as a productive
emphatic pattern built on the ordinary pronoun paradigm.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:emph-keimah}{}
\reviewobjectline{Kain in Topa' tungah, Keimah gim hong kipiakna, ka thuak zawh ding hi lo hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
kei-mah \\
\textsc{1sg}-\textsc{emph} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'And Cain said unto the LORD, My punishment is greater than I can bear.'}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:emph-nangmah}{}
\reviewobjectline{Abimelek in, En in, ka leitang, na mai-ah om hi. Nangmah in hoih na sakna munah teng in, a ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
nang-mah \\
\textsc{2sg}-\textsc{emph} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'And Abimelech said, Behold, my land is before thee: dwell where it pleaseth thee.'}
\end{reviewexample}

The same element also appears in negative-indefinite forms such as
\texttt{kuamah} `no one' and \texttt{bangmah} `nothing'
\citep[sec.~3.2.7]{zamngaihcing2017}. Those items belong more naturally
to a later section on indefinites and interrogative-based forms, but the
connection should already be noted here.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Reflexive and reciprocal marking with
\texttt{ki-}}{Reflexive and reciprocal marking with ki-}}\label{reflexive-and-reciprocal-marking-with-ki-}

Zam Ngaih Cing describes reflexive pronouns as repeated pronouns linked
by \texttt{leh} \citep[sec.~3.2.5]{zamngaihcing2017}. The biblical
corpus nevertheless makes it clear that a print grammar also needs to
acknowledge verbal \texttt{ki-}, since many reader-facing examples of
reflexive or reciprocal meaning are expressed through \texttt{ki-} forms
rather than through a free reflexive pronoun alone.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:refl-ki}{}
\reviewobjectline{Tua thu hangin pasal in a nu le a pa nusia-in a zi tawh kigawm a, amau tegel pum khat a bang uh hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
ki-gawm \\
\textsc{refl}-join \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife: and they shall be one flesh.'}
\end{reviewexample}

For print purposes, \texttt{ki-} can already be described as a
productive reflexive or reciprocal verbal prefix. The remaining caution
is lexical rather than structural: some surface \texttt{ki-} forms are
lexicalized stems, so not every \texttt{ki-} word should be taken
automatically as transparent reflexive morphology.

\subsubsection{Pronominal prefixes and inverse/directional
marking}\label{pronominal-prefixes-and-inversedirectional-marking}

Henderson's discussion of pronominal concord is still the best starting
point for the bound prefix system. She pairs \texttt{kei} with
\texttt{ka-}, \texttt{nang} with \texttt{na-}, the \texttt{ei/eite}
series with \texttt{i-}, the \texttt{ko/kote} series with \texttt{ka-},
\texttt{no/note} with \texttt{na-}, and all other nominals with
\texttt{a-} \citep[32-33]{henderson1965}. Zam Ngaih Cing's discussion of
nominal prefixes confirms the same basic person-marking inventory in
noun phrases \citep[sec.~3.3.4.1.1]{zamngaihcing2017}. A printed chapter
on pronouns does not need the whole verbal paradigm yet, but it does
need to note that the independent pronouns belong to a wider system of
bound person marking.

The harder question concerns \texttt{hong-} and \texttt{kong-}. The
literature treats them as participant-oriented preverbal prefixes with
directional or inverse-like behavior, and Otsuka's causative discussion
shows that first- and second-person objects are structurally important
to their distribution
\citetext{\citealp[sec.~5.8.1.3]{zamngaihcing2017}; \citealp{otsuka_causative}}.
The current corpus outputs, however, do not yet yield a stable automatic
example set for \texttt{hong-}, so the present slice uses only narrow,
manually checked illustrations.\footnote{The present editorial problem
  is not whether these forms exist, but how narrowly they should be
  defined in a first print chapter. The current wording stays close to
  cases that can be read directly from the verse context without relying
  on a fully solved backend analysis.}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:hong-prefix}{}
\reviewobjectline{Amah in a sangna panin a khut tawh zamin kei hong la a, tui thukpi panin keimah hong kaikhia hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
hong & la \\
3>1 & take \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'He sent from above, he took me, He drew me out of many waters.'}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:kong-prefix}{}
\reviewobjectline{Faro in Josef' kiangah, Egypt gam khempeuh a uk dingin nang kong koih khinzo hi, a ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
kong & koih \\
1>2 & set \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'And Pharaoh said unto Joseph, See, I have set thee over all the land of Egypt.'}
\end{reviewexample}

These examples are enough to justify a cautious print description:
\texttt{hong-} and \texttt{kong-} belong with person-sensitive preverbal
marking, but the exact boundary between inverse, venitive, benefactive,
and wider directional readings still needs fuller chapter-level review.
They should therefore appear in the dictionary slice and grammar prose,
but under an explicitly provisional analysis rather than as a closed
paradigm.

\subsubsection{Editorial summary}\label{editorial-summary-1}

This slice now supports a real draft chapter on pronouns and pronominal
marking. Independent pronouns, emphatic forms in \texttt{-mah}, and
possessive prefixes are straightforward enough for print. \texttt{ki-}
is also clear enough to present as reflexive or reciprocal verbal
marking, provided lexicalized forms remain a review checkpoint. The main
unresolved issue is not the existence of clusivity or of
\texttt{hong-/kong-}, but how far the current report layer can be
trusted to label those patterns automatically without closer editorial
control.

\subsubsection{Scope}\label{scope-2}

\texttt{sung}, \texttt{kiang}, and \texttt{laizang}.

The present print-facing examples are controlled by
\texttt{candidates\_case\_marking.tsv} and interpreted in
\texttt{dossier\_case\_marking.md}. This keeps the slice narrow: the
packet now prints candidate-backed anchors and explicit caveats rather
than broadening into a new full chapter or a broad automatic case-marker
survey.

\subsubsection{Case marking in outline}\label{case-marking-in-outline}

Earlier descriptions agree that Tedim marks nominal relations after the
noun phrase, but they differ in how they analyze those forms. Henderson
describes them structurally as phrase-final or post-nominal particles
rather than as a semantic case system
\citetext{\citealp[59]{henderson1965}; \citealp[104]{henderson1965}}.
Zam Ngaih Cing, by contrast, offers a seven-case analysis with explicit
ergative, locative, ablative, and comitative categories
\citep[sec.~3.3.3.3]{zamngaihcing2017}. Comparative Sukte evidence
points in the same direction for several core markers, even though Singh
labels the cognate \texttt{-in} as nominative rather than ergative
\citep[sec.~4.5.1]{sukte_grammar}.

For a printed grammar, the most useful generalization is therefore
twofold. First, the language is well described by a case-marking
analysis in the modern sense. Second, the most natural corpus examples
often combine case markers with relational nominal stems, so a final
chapter should not force an artificial boundary between ``case
suffixes'' and ``spatial nouns''.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Ergative
\texttt{-in}}{Ergative -in}}\label{ergative--in}

The ergative marker \texttt{-in} marks the transitive subject in Tedim
Chin \citep[sec.~3.3.3.3.1]{zamngaihcing2017}. Henderson does not use
the label \emph{ergative}, but her treatment of phrase-final nominal
particles is compatible with the later analysis
\citep[59]{henderson1965}. Otsuka likewise assumes ergative \texttt{-in}
in discussing causers in causative constructions
\citep{otsuka_causative}. Comparative Sukte retains a cognate
\texttt{-in} marker, though Singh describes it as nominative
\citep[sec.~4.5.1]{sukte_grammar}.

In this slice, Genesis 4:3 remains the accepted print anchor because
\texttt{candidates\_case\_marking.tsv} and
\texttt{dossier\_case\_marking.md} both treat \texttt{Kain\ in} as the
cleanest candidate-backed ergative window. Raw \texttt{-in} extraction
is unsafe: rows such as \texttt{ciangin} are conjunctional or other
non-case material, so they must not be promoted as ergative case
examples.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:erg-in}{}
\reviewobjectline{Tua hun sungin Kain in lei pana piang gah piakna Topa' tungah paipih a,}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
Kain & in \\
Cain & \textsc{erg} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{‘And in process of time it came to pass, that Cain brought of the fruit of the ground an offering unto the LORD.’}
\end{reviewexample}

This example is sufficient for a draft chapter because it shows the
essential pattern without relying on doubtful segmentation. The agent
phrase \texttt{Kain\ in} is simple, animate, and immediately followed by
a clearly transitive predicate. At the same time, the section remains
cautious: one accepted candidate-backed example is enough to print the
analysis, but additional \texttt{-in} rows should still be filtered
against ambiguity controls such as \texttt{ciangin} before they are
treated as nominal case evidence.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Locative
\texttt{-ah}}{Locative -ah}}\label{locative--ah}

The locative \texttt{-ah} marks location
\citep[sec.~3.3.3.3.3]{zamngaihcing2017}. Henderson already treats
\texttt{-ah} as a locative post-nominal particle and discusses its
phonological behavior in locative nominal figures
\citep[54-56]{henderson1965}. The current candidate layer, however,
distinguishes plain noun-plus-locative evidence such as \texttt{khua-ah}
from relator-noun-plus-case constructions such as \texttt{laizangah},
\texttt{vantungah}, \texttt{kiangah}, \texttt{sungah}, and
\texttt{tungah}. The printed description should therefore be slightly
richer than a simple gloss ``at/in'', while still keeping those two
subtypes distinct.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:loc-ah}{}
\reviewobjectline{Pasian in, “Tuite' laizangah van kuumpi om hen la, tua van kuumpi in tui le tui kikhensak hen,” ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
lai-zang-ah \\
middle-side-\textsc{loc} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{‘And God said, Let there be a firmament in the midst of the waters, and let it divide the waters from the waters.’}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:loc-ah-2}{}
\reviewobjectline{leitung khua a vaksak dingin vantungah khuavak hi uh hen,” ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
vantung-ah \\
heaven-\textsc{loc} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{‘and let them be for lights in the firmament of the heaven to give light upon the earth’}
\end{reviewexample}

The first example shows especially clearly that \texttt{-ah} often
completes a larger relational expression rather than attaching only to a
simple lexical noun. The noun \texttt{laizang} contributes the spatial
geometry `middle, interior region', and \texttt{-ah} marks that whole
phrase as locative. The second printed example is still best treated in
the same relator-noun-plus-case domain, even if \texttt{vantung} looks
less obviously relational than \texttt{laizang}. The plain
noun-plus-locative control in the candidate layer is \texttt{khua-ah},
not one of these relator rows.

The analyzer export also needs to be read cautiously here. Some locative
and relator rows surface with \texttt{pos\_span=FUNC}, even though the
grammar still treats the base as nominal or relational. That export
label is useful metadata, but it is not decisive enough to collapse
noun-like and relator-like rows into a single undifferentiated suffix
list.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Directional/allative
\texttt{-a}}{Directional/allative -a}}\label{directionalallative--a}

Directional or allative \texttt{-a} remains deferred in the current
packet. The candidate layer keeps \texttt{-a} separate from
\texttt{-ah}, and the dossier is explicit that the present export does
not yet distinguish directional \texttt{-a} cleanly from pronominal or
other functional \texttt{a} tokens. For that reason, the current slice
does \textbf{not} collapse \texttt{-a} into \texttt{-ah}, and it does
not yet print a dedicated \texttt{-a} example.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Source marking: \texttt{-pan} and
\texttt{-panin}}{Source marking: -pan and -panin}}\label{source-marking--pan-and--panin}

Zam Ngaih Cing describes \texttt{-pan} as the ablative marker indicating
source or point of departure \citep[sec.~3.3.3.3.5]{zamngaihcing2017}.
Comparative Sukte has a cognate \texttt{-pan}, which strengthens the
historical plausibility of the analysis \citep{sukte_grammar}. Henderson
does not present an ablative category in the modern sense, but the
source-marking behavior is nevertheless compatible with her structural
account of post-nominal particles \citep[104]{henderson1965}.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:pan}{}
\reviewobjectline{Tua ahih manin hih thukhamte lakpan a neupente khat bek nangawn zuikha loin, midangte in zong a zuih loh nadingin a gen mite pen vantung ki-ukna sungah mi neupen hi ding uh hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
lak-pan \\
midst-\textsc{abl} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{‘Whosoever therefore shall break one of these least commandments, and shall teach men so, he shall be called the least in the kingdom of heaven.’}
\end{reviewexample}

This example is worth keeping because it shows a natural source
construction built on a relator noun. The source relation is real, but
it is not expressed through a bare noun alone: \texttt{lak} `among, in
the midst of' combines with \texttt{-pan} to yield a source phrase
meaning `from among'. The current candidate layer therefore treats
\texttt{lakpan} as source marking on a relator noun, not merely as a
bare suffix example.

The related form \texttt{-panin} is common in the corpus and clearly
belongs to the same source-marking domain, especially after relator
nouns and other spatial expressions. What remains uncertain is not its
source meaning, but its exact structural status in every occurrence.
Some examples look like straightforward source phrases, while others
behave more like extended or tightly fused source-marking expressions.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:panin}{}
\reviewobjectline{Topa in Abram kiangah, “Na gam, na beh, le na pa' inn panin nang kong lah ding gamah pai in.”}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
inn & panin \\
house & from \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{‘Now the LORD had said unto Abram, Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father’s house.’}
\end{reviewexample}

For that reason, a printed grammar can already gloss \texttt{-panin} as
a source form related to \texttt{-pan}, but should avoid a stronger
compositional claim until the full range of corpus contexts has been
reviewed more closely. The analyzer segmentation \texttt{pan-in} is
useful evidence for the packet, but it is not by itself a final
structural analysis.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Comitative
\texttt{-tawh}}{Comitative -tawh}}\label{comitative--tawh}

The comitative \texttt{-tawh} is described in the literature as the
marker of accompaniment \citep[sec.~3.3.3.3.6]{zamngaihcing2017}.
Henderson also lists \texttt{tawh} among the post-nominal particles
\citep[104]{henderson1965}. In the biblical corpus, however, the most
accessible examples are semantically broader than simple human
accompaniment. A printed chapter should therefore distinguish the core
comitative use from its material and instrumental extensions rather than
treating all examples as equivalent.\footnote{The currently available
  biblical examples for \texttt{-tawh} are semantically good enough for
  review, but they do not all illustrate the most prototypical
  accompaniment reading. That is why the present slice pairs one
  accompaniment example with one extension example rather than treating
  Genesis 2:7 or Genesis 2:21 as if they were ordinary companion
  phrases.}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:tawh-accompaniment}{}
\reviewobjectline{Tangvalte' neksate le kei tawh hong kuan mite' tanh dingte lobuang bangmah ka la kei ding hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
kei & tawh \\
\textsc{1sg} & \textsc{com} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{‘save only that which the young men have eaten, and the portion of the men which went with me’}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:tawh-extension}{}
\reviewobjectline{Topa Pasian in leilak pana leivui tawh mihing bawl a, a nak sungah nuntakna hu sang suk hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}ll@{}}
leivui & tawh \\
dust & \textsc{com} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{‘And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life.’}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 14:24 illustrates the core accompaniment use well enough for
print: \texttt{kei\ tawh} marks the men as companions who went with
Abram. Genesis 2:7, by contrast, shows a broader use in which
\texttt{-tawh} marks material or means. That wider range should remain
explicit in the grammar and stay split in the same way it is split in
the candidate layer. The comitative meaning remains central, but the
marker extends into associated material and instrument-like readings in
ways that the English gloss \emph{with} only partly captures.

\subsubsection{Relator nouns}\label{relator-nouns}

The locative and source examples already show that Tedim spatial grammar
is not exhausted by a list of case markers. Relator nouns such as
\texttt{lak}, \texttt{sung}, \texttt{kiang}, \texttt{tung},
\texttt{laizang}, and \texttt{vantung} regularly host locative and
ablative marking, and they do so at very high frequency in the
candidate-backed packet. Henderson's structural treatment makes room for
this by analyzing many such forms in terms of nominal figures rather
than sharply separated case suffixes \citep[59]{henderson1965}. Zam
Ngaih Cing's case system, meanwhile, makes the semantic contribution of
the markers clearer \citep[sec.~3.3.3.3]{zamngaihcing2017}.

For a final chapter, the best solution will probably be to treat the
system in two layers: first the case markers themselves, then the class
of relational nouns that commonly host them. The present review slice
stops short of a full relator-noun section, but it already makes clear
that the two layers belong in the same part of the grammar and should
not be flattened into a bare suffix list.

\subsubsection{Editorial summary}\label{editorial-summary-2}

This slice now supports a modest but genuine draft chapter. Ergative
\texttt{-in} can be described with a manually confirmed biblical
example; \texttt{-ah} and \texttt{-pan} are already straightforward to
illustrate; \texttt{-panin} can be described conservatively as a related
source form; and \texttt{-tawh} can be presented with one clear
accompaniment example plus one extension example. The remaining work is
not to broaden the chapter, but to decide how fully the final grammar
should integrate relator nouns into the same printed discussion.

\subsubsection{Relator nouns as relational
hosts}\label{relator-nouns-as-relational-hosts}

The first-slice relator-noun anchors are \texttt{kiang}, \texttt{lak},
\texttt{sung}, \texttt{tung}, and cautiously \texttt{pualam}. These are
best treated as relational nouns or relational stems that can host
locative or source marking rather than as simple bare case suffixes.

\texttt{Kiang} is the clearest `beside / near / at the side of' anchor.
\texttt{Lak} is the clearest `among / amid / between' anchor.
\texttt{Sung} is the clearest `inside / within / among' anchor.
\texttt{Tung} is usable for `on / above / upon', but it stays slightly
closer to case-marking territory than the first three anchors and should
therefore keep an explicit caveat. \texttt{Pualam} is usable for
`outside / exterior side', but it should remain the most cautious
positive relator-noun anchor in the first slice because it still looks
somewhat more lexical-nominal than \texttt{kiang}, \texttt{lak},
\texttt{sung}, or \texttt{tung}.

The point of this slice is not to deny the role of \texttt{-ah} or
\texttt{-pan}. It is to keep visible that forms such as \texttt{kiang},
\texttt{lak}, \texttt{sung}, \texttt{tung}, and \texttt{pualam}
contribute relational-host structure before locative or source marking
is added. They are not simply bare case suffixes in disguise.

\subsubsection{Separate and relator-hosted
postpositions}\label{separate-and-relator-hosted-postpositions}

The first-slice postpositional material stays deliberately narrow.
\texttt{Pan} is usable only as a separate or relator-hosted source
postposition, as in \texttt{kiang\ pan} or \texttt{lak\ pan}.
\texttt{Panin} is usable only as a source form with structural caution.
\texttt{Tawh} is usable only as a separate accompaniment or associative
postposition, and it must keep its case-marking boundary caveat visible.

This slice therefore treats \texttt{pan}, \texttt{panin}, and
\texttt{tawh} only where they are clearly separate or clearly
relator-hosted. It does \textbf{not} replace the existing case-marking
treatment of \texttt{-ah}, \texttt{-in}, \texttt{-pan}, \texttt{-panin},
or \texttt{-tawh}. The goal is to show how relator nouns and separate
postpositions meet in the same constructions without reopening the
settled case-marking packet.

\texttt{Tawhin} remains deferred or boundary-only in the first slice.
The current evidence is sparse and strongly instrument-like, so it is
not yet a clean postpositional anchor parallel to \texttt{pan},
\texttt{panin}, or \texttt{tawh}.

\subsubsection{Case-marking boundary}\label{case-marking-boundary}

Attached or fused-looking rows such as \texttt{kiangah},
\texttt{sungah}, \texttt{tungah}, \texttt{lakpan}, and similar forms are
shared boundary territory with the case-marking packet. They are useful
here because they show the relational-host side of the system, but they
are not a reason to reopen the case-marking analysis or to treat every
attached-looking form as an independent postposition.

This is the main value of the current grammar slice. The case-marking
packet already explains the marker side of the system. The
relators/postpositions slice adds value by explaining the
relational-host side: why \texttt{kiang}, \texttt{lak}, \texttt{sung},
\texttt{tung}, and \texttt{pualam} matter, and why separate or
relator-hosted \texttt{pan}, \texttt{panin}, and \texttt{tawh} should
not be flattened into a broad inventory of suffix-like strings.

\subsubsection{Deferred and boundary
material}\label{deferred-and-boundary-material}

Several items stay outside the first grammar slice as core claims.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \texttt{nuai} remains lower-confidence boundary material rather than a
  first-slice relator-noun anchor.
\item
  \texttt{mai} remains lower-confidence boundary material because it
  stays close to lexical noun or body-part uses.
\item
  \texttt{tawhin} remains deferred or boundary-only because the current
  evidence is sparse and instrument-like.
\item
  Raw report counts are not evidence for the slice.
\item
  \texttt{kipan} and \texttt{kipanin} rows involving \texttt{ki} remain

\item
  Attached or fused-looking forms should not be promoted here as
  independent postpositions just because they contain \texttt{pan},
  \texttt{panin}, or locative material.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Numerals}\label{numerals}

\emph{Source slice:
\texttt{output/publication\_review/grammar\_numerals\_print\_slice.md}}

\subsubsection{Scope}\label{scope-3}

This is a short print-facing draft section on numerals in Tedim Chin,
controlled by \texttt{candidates\_numerals.tsv} and
\texttt{dossier\_numerals.md}. It covers only a small candidate-backed
set: basic cardinal counting phrases, compound tens, one ordinal form,
one occurrence-counting or multiplicative form, one large-number phrase

It does not yet attempt a full numeral paradigm, a full classifier
system, a full account of distributive reduplication, quantifiers, or
generated-report frequency tables. Dictionary and review-note slices
have not yet begun.

\subsubsection{Numerals in outline}\label{numerals-in-outline}

The current candidate-backed packet supports a narrow generalization.
Simple cardinals can follow a counted noun in examples such as
\texttt{kum\ nih} and \texttt{ni\ sagih}. Compound tens are represented
by \texttt{sawmkua}, ordinal formation is represented by \texttt{nihna},
occurrence-counting is represented by \texttt{sawmvei}, and large
biblical number expressions are visible but need caveats. Across the
packet, \texttt{kua} and \texttt{khat} require constructional controls
rather than raw-string treatment.

\subsubsection{Basic cardinal counting
phrases}\label{basic-cardinal-counting-phrases}

The cleanest basic counting phrases in the current packet are Genesis
11:10 and Genesis 7:10.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:num-kum-nih}{}
\reviewobjectline{kum nih}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
kum & | & nih \\
year & | & two \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'two years'}
\end{reviewexample}

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:num-ni-sagih}{}
\reviewobjectline{ni sagih}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
ni & | & sagih \\

\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'seven days'}
\end{reviewexample}

These are clean post-nominal counting phrases. The printed claim should

language.

\subsubsection{Compound tens}\label{compound-tens}

Genesis 5:9 gives the clearest current compound-ten example:

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:num-sawmkua}{}
\reviewobjectline{kum sawmkua}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
kum & | & sawm-kua \\
year & | & ten-nine \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'ninety years'}
\end{reviewexample}

\texttt{Sawmkua} matters for two reasons. First, it shows a compound-ten
structure. Second, it is the strongest current numeral-side control for
\texttt{kua\ =\ nine}. The dossier also keeps one analyzer/export caveat
visible: the segmentation and gloss support the compound reading, but
the lemma and POS layer is flattened (\texttt{kum\ \textbar{}\ sawm},
\texttt{N\ \textbar{}\ N}). That caveat should stay in the prose rather
than being silently normalized away.

\subsubsection{Ordinals}\label{ordinals}

Genesis 7:11 supplies the current ordinal anchor:

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:num-nihna}{}
\reviewobjectline{nihna}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
nih-na \\
two-\textsc{nmlz} \\
\end{tabular}

\end{reviewexample}

\texttt{Nihna} is the current candidate-backed ordinal example. The
generated report mentions \texttt{masa} `first', but \texttt{masa} is
not promoted in this slice, and this section does not attempt a full
ordinal paradigm. The dossier also records that \texttt{pos\_span\ =\ N}
in the export; that is a label caveat, not a reason to reject the
ordinal analysis.

\subsubsection{Occurrence counting / multiplicative

Genesis 31:7 keeps one compact occurrence-counting form in view:

\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}l@{}}
sawm-vei \\
ten-times \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'ten times'}
\end{reviewexample}

The generated report paraphrases this as \texttt{vei\ sawm}, but the

occurrence-counting visible, but it is not enough to build a full
classifier system.

\subsubsection{Large-number phrase}\label{large-number-phrase}

Genesis 5:27 gives one usable large-number phrase, but only with
explicit caveats:

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:num-large}{}
\reviewwarninginline{[REVIEW PREVIEW WARNING: segmentation/gloss token mismatch in ex:num-large; showing padded alignment.]}
\reviewobjectline{kum zakua le kum sawmguk le kua}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lllllllllllllll@{}}
kum & | & za-kua & | & le & | & kum & | & sawm-guk & | & le & | & kua &  &  \\
year & | & hundred-nine & | & and & | & year & | & ten-six & | & and & | & nine & [export: & who] \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'nine hundred sixty and nine years'}
\end{reviewexample}

This construction is numeral, not interrogative. The dossier is

\texttt{za-kua}, and the final \texttt{kua} is glossed as \texttt{who}
in the candidate TSV even though the constructional context is numeral.
The print gloss therefore keeps \texttt{nine} reader-facing while
marking the export gloss in the example line. This makes the row usable
with caveat, but not a fully polished anchor for a larger large-number
section.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\texttt{Kua}
ambiguity}{Kua ambiguity}}\label{kua-ambiguity}

\texttt{Kua} needs an explicit control note in the printed grammar. In
constructionally numeral contexts such as \texttt{sawmkua} and the
Genesis 5:27 large-number phrase, \texttt{kua} is numeral \texttt{nine}.
In interrogative clauses, however, \texttt{kua} is also \texttt{who}.

The blocked control in the numerals packet is Genesis 48:8:

\begin{quote}
Hihte kua ahi hiam?
\end{quote}

That row belongs to the interrogatives packet, not to numerals. Future
numerals prose must therefore not use raw \texttt{kua} hits as numeral
evidence.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\texttt{Khat} and the numeral/indefinite
boundary}{Khat and the numeral/indefinite boundary}}\label{khat-and-the-numeralindefinite-boundary}

Genesis 32:24 gives a useful but boundary-marked example:

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:num-mi-khat}{}
\reviewobjectline{mi khat}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
mi & | & khat \\
person & | & one \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{'a man' / 'one person'}
\end{reviewexample}

\texttt{Mi\ khat} is analyzer-backed and worth keeping visible, but its
English context ``a man'' shows why it should not be treated as an
uncomplicated bare numeral \texttt{one} example. The packet therefore
keeps it as numeral/indefinite boundary evidence and does not start a
quantifiers retrofit here.

\subsubsection{Distributive reduplication
deferred}\label{distributive-reduplication-deferred}

The generated report claims distributive \texttt{sagih\ sagih} for `by
sevens', but the candidate layer keeps that material deferred. In the
current analyzer export window, Genesis 7:2 preserves only a single
\texttt{sagih} token. Distributive reduplication therefore remains not
print-ready in this slice, and \texttt{sagih\ sagih} should not be
printed as though it were already analyzer-backed.

\subsubsection{Editorial summary}\label{editorial-summary-3}

This slice now safely supports six modest claims: noun-plus-numeral
counting examples are printable; compound-ten \texttt{sawmkua} is
candidate-backed; ordinal \texttt{nihna} is candidate-backed;
occurrence-counting \texttt{sawmvei} remains visible with a fused-export
caveat; one large-number phrase is usable with explicit analyzer
caveats; and the packet has explicit controls for \texttt{kua} and
\texttt{khat}.

What remains deferred is equally important: raw frequency counts, full
numeral paradigms, \texttt{masa}, distributive reduplication, a full
classifier system, quantifier overlap, and raw \texttt{kua} matching.
The next step after this grammar slice is the dictionary print slice,
while review-note work has not yet begun.

\paragraph{The Form I / Form II

\subsection{Negation}\label{negation}

\emph{Source slice:
\texttt{output/publication\_review/grammar\_negation\_print\_slice.md}}

\subsubsection{Scope}\label{scope-6}

This account offers a short treatment of Tedim negation. It is
intentionally narrow. It focuses on a small set of manually checked
Bible examples and on the interaction between \texttt{lo}, \texttt{loh},
and \texttt{kei}. It does not attempt a full TAM chapter, a treatment of
directionals, or a complete account of every negative construction in
the corpus.

\subsubsection{Overview of the negation
system}\label{overview-of-the-negation-system}

The literature and the Bible corpus agree on one central point: Tedim
negation cannot be reduced to \texttt{lo} alone. Henderson foregrounds
\texttt{-kei}, including prohibitive uses, while Zam Ngaih Cing
explicitly describes a contrast between \texttt{-kei} and
\texttt{-lou/-louh} and links the latter to stem-sensitive or dependent
environments \citep{henderson1965, zamngaihcing2017}. The safest summary
is therefore not ``Tedim negation = \texttt{lo}'', but rather that the
present corpus supports a three-part treatment: ordinary clause-level
negation with \texttt{lo}, dependent or derived negation with
\texttt{loh}, and \texttt{kei} as a central negator in prohibitives and
many irrealis-heavy or directive contexts.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Clause-level negation with
\texttt{lo}}{Clause-level negation with lo}}\label{clause-level-negation-with-lo}

\texttt{Lo} is the safest place to start because it is the clearest
ordinary clause-level negator in the Bible corpus. It appears in
straightforward negative predicates such as \texttt{thusim\ lo\ hi},
\texttt{nei\ lo\ hi}, \texttt{om\ lo\ hi}, and in many future or modal
combinations such as \texttt{V\ lo\ ding}. That does not make it the
whole negation system, but it does make it the most transparent starting
point.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:neg-lo}{Genesis 4:5}
\reviewobjectline{ahih hangin Kain le ama piakna thusim lo hi. Tua ahih ciangin Kain heh mahmah a, a mai sia hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
thusim & lo & hi \\
accept & \textsc{neg} & \textsc{decl} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"but unto Cain and to his offering he had not respect. And Cain was very wroth, and his countenance fell."}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 4:5 is a good central example because it shows exactly the
clause type a reader expects from a first negation section: a verbal
predicate followed by \texttt{lo} and the clause-final declarative
material. Genesis 11:30 \texttt{ta\ nei\ lo\ hi} is equally useful in a
more compact predicative clause, and existential patterns such as
\texttt{om\ lo\ hi} belong in the same zone.

\texttt{V\ lo\ uh} is a real and common surface string, but it is not
automatically prohibitive. Genesis 2:25 \texttt{maizum\ lo\ uh\ hi}
means ``they were not ashamed'' and is simply an ordinary plural
negative predicate. Similar plural negatives occur elsewhere with no
imperative force. It is better treated as an ordinary clausal negative
pattern than as the defining prohibitive construction.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{Dependent and derived negation with
\texttt{loh}}{Dependent and derived negation with loh}}\label{dependent-and-derived-negation-with-loh}

\texttt{Loh} should not be treated as a random spelling variant of
\texttt{lo}. In the current Bible corpus it clusters in dependent,
purposive, and derived environments such as \texttt{...\ loh\ dinga},
\texttt{...\ loh\ dingin}, and \texttt{...\ loh\ nadingin}. The safest
label is therefore something like ``dependent or derived negative
form'', not just ``another way to spell \texttt{lo}''.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:neg-loh}{Genesis 3:11}
\reviewobjectline{Amah in, “Na guaktanga na omna kua in hong gen ahi hiam? Na nek loh dinga kong thupiak singgah ne kha na hi hiam?” ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
nek & loh & ding-a \\
eat.\textsc{ii} & \textsc{neg}.\textsc{dep} & \textsc{irr}-\textsc{erg} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And he said, Who told thee that thou wast naked? Hast thou eaten of the tree, whereof I commanded thee that thou shouldest not eat?"}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 3:11 is especially useful because \texttt{loh} is not just
attached to a bare verb in isolation. It appears inside a dependent
complex, \texttt{na\ nek\ loh\ dinga\ kong\ thupiak}, where the whole
point is the commanded non-occurrence of the event. Isaiah 6:10
strengthens the same pattern by repeating \texttt{muh\ loh\ nading},
\texttt{zak\ loh\ nading}, and \texttt{theihtel\ loh\ nading} inside
clearly purposive or clause-linking material. This is exactly the
distribution seen again and again in the corpus: \texttt{loh} is where
the Bible orthography most visibly tracks non-finite, dependent, and
derived negation.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{\texttt{kei} in prohibitives and
irrealis-heavy
negation}{kei in prohibitives and irrealis-heavy negation}}\label{kei-in-prohibitives-and-irrealis-heavy-negation}

\texttt{Kei} is central to real prohibitives in the Bible corpus, and
those prohibitives are among the cleanest manually checked negative
examples available. This alone is enough to show why the negation system
cannot be flattened to \texttt{lo}.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:neg-kei}{Genesis 22:12}
\reviewobjectline{Amah in, “Tangvalpa su kei in. Ama tungah bangmah hih kei in. Bang hang hiam cih leh nang in Pasian na zahtakna tu-in ka thei a, na tapa khat neihsun keima a dingin na humcip lohna ka mu hi,” a ci hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lllllll@{}}
su & kei & in & ... & hih & kei & in \\
strike & \textsc{neg} & \textsc{imp} & ... & do & \textsc{neg} & \textsc{imp} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And he said, Lay not thine hand upon the lad, neither do thou any thing unto him: for now I know that thou fearest God, seeing thou hast not withheld thy son, thine only son from me."}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 22:12 gives two clear negative imperatives in the same speech
turn. Genesis 15:1 \texttt{Lau\ kei\ in}, Genesis 19:17
\texttt{Nunghei\ kei\ unla,\ ...\ khawl\ kei\ un}, Leviticus 10:9
\texttt{ne\ kei\ un}, and Numbers 14:42
\texttt{kuanto\ kei\ un\ ...\ do\ kei\ un} all point in the same
direction: \texttt{kei} is the core prohibitive negator in the current
Bible data.

The corpus also shows that \texttt{kei} is not limited to prohibitives
or to first-person realis. Exodus 5:2 has both
\texttt{ka\ thei\ kei\ hi} and \texttt{ka\ paisak\ kei\ ding\ hi}, which
shows \texttt{kei} in ordinary negation and in irrealis-heavy future
material. The safest print summary is therefore that \texttt{kei} is
central in prohibitives and common in directive, quoted, or otherwise
irrealis-heavy negation, without forcing the whole system into a rigid
person-based rule.

\subsubsection{Negative existence and cessative
negation}\label{negative-existence-and-cessative-negation}

Negative existence belongs with ordinary \texttt{lo}-based negation, but
it deserves its own short section because it is extremely common and
very readable in Bible prose. Clauses such as Genesis 11:30
\texttt{ta\ nei\ lo\ hi}, Genesis 37:24
\texttt{a\ sungah\ tui\ om\ lo\ hi}, and Genesis 39:9
\texttt{a\ kep\ tuam\ bangmah\ om\ lo\ hi} show the ordinary Tedim way
of saying that something is absent, unavailable, or not possessed.

The corpus also supports a neat constructional entry for
\texttt{nawn\ lo} ``no longer, not again''. Genesis 8:12
\texttt{hong\ ciahkik\ nawn\ lo\ hi} is a clean cessative example, and
Genesis 17:5 \texttt{Na\ min\ Abram\ hi\ nawn\ lo\ ding\ a} shows the
same construction in a renamed-future environment. This material is
better treated as a construction than as a new basic negator.

\subsubsection{Ability and inability}\label{ability-and-inability}

The clearest treatment here is not a bare negator but the constructional
pair \texttt{thei\ lo\ /\ theih\ loh}. The Bible shows common
clause-level inability with \texttt{thei\ lo}, while dependent or
derived negative ability is better represented by \texttt{theih\ loh}
patterns than by the much rarer exact string \texttt{theih\ lo}.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:neg-thei-lo}{Genesis 27:23}
\reviewobjectline{A khutte in a sanggampa Esau’ khut bangin mul nei ahih manin amah in thei lo hi. Tua ahih ciangin amah in thupha pia dingin kithawi a,}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
thei & lo & hi \\
know & \textsc{neg} & \textsc{decl} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And he discerned him not, because his hands were hairy, as his brother Esau's hands: so he blessed him."}
\end{reviewexample}

Genesis 27:23 is a compact ordinary example: \texttt{thei\ lo\ hi} means
that recognition or knowledge failed to occur. Genesis 37:4
\texttt{amah\ hopih\ thei\ lo\ uh\ hi} shows the same pattern with an
ability reading, while Exodus 33:20 \texttt{na\ mu\ thei\ kei\ ding\ hi}
shows inability under \texttt{kei}. On the dependent side, Exodus 10:5
\texttt{muh\ theih\ loh\ nadingin} and Isaiah 6:10
\texttt{theihtel\ loh\ nading} show why \texttt{theih\ loh} must be
represented directly rather than treating exact \texttt{theih\ lo} as
the dominant form in the current corpus.

\subsubsection{Negative polarity items}\label{negative-polarity-items}

The Bible corpus gives clean negative-polarity material, but only after
filtering. Raw exact-string counts overgenerate because \texttt{kuamah}
can occur in non-pronominal strings and \texttt{bangmah} has non-NPI
uses such as \texttt{tua\ bangmah\ hi-in} ``likewise''. The discussion
here therefore uses only manually checked examples.

\begin{reviewexample}{ex:neg-kuamah}{Exodus 2:12}
\reviewobjectline{Amah khuadak kawikawi a, kuamah mu lo ahih manin Egypt mipa thatin sehnel sungah a seel hi.}
\begin{tabular}[t]{@{}lll@{}}
kuamah & mu & lo \\
nobody & see & \textsc{neg} \\
\end{tabular}
\reviewtranslation{"And he looked this way and that way, and when he saw that there was no man, he slew the Egyptian, and hid him in the sand."}
\end{reviewexample}

Exodus 2:12 is a good \texttt{kuamah} example because the NPI is clearly
licensed by the negative predicate. \texttt{Bangmah} behaves similarly
in real NPI contexts such as Genesis 22:12
\texttt{Ama\ tungah\ bangmah\ hih\ kei\ in} and Genesis 39:9
\texttt{bangmah\ om\ lo\ hi}. Those are the kinds of examples the
dictionary can safely quote. What should stay out of the main prose is
any claim based on raw counts alone.

\subsubsection{Summary}\label{summary}

The current evidence is strong enough for a stable negation chapter, but
only if it keeps the system narrow and honest. \texttt{Lo} is the safest
starting point for ordinary clause-level negation. \texttt{Loh} is a
real dependent or derived negative form and should not be collapsed into
accidental spelling variation. \texttt{Kei} is central in prohibitives
and in many irrealis-heavy negatives, so the chapter must represent it
directly rather than relegating it to a footnote.

Just as important, the description must avoid false simplicity.
\texttt{V\ lo\ uh} is not the prohibitive construction, Genesis 2:25 is
not a prohibitive example, and the NPI section must use filtered
examples rather than raw exact-string totals. The result is a compact
but linguistically honest account of the current Bible evidence.
