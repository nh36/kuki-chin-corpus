# What works

This packet is now strong enough to show that verb stem alternation can be handled as a real print-facing topic rather than as a backend memo. The grammar slice reads as continuous prose, starts from the literature-backed Form I / Form II contrast, and then narrows quickly to a small set of manually checked Bible examples. That is the right editorial strategy for this topic, because the existence of the alternation is secure even when the report layer is still uneven.

The literature and the corpus agree on the core point. Henderson and Zam Ngaih Cing clearly describe a two-form system, and the current corpus/paradigm outputs confirm that paired stems such as `mu/muh`, `ne/nek`, `nei/neih`, `thei/theih`, `piang/pian`, and `pia/piak` are genuinely present in Tedim. The strongest examples are the ones that show Form I in finite predication and Form II in dependent or nominalized environments rather than relying on abstract labels alone.

The chosen examples are safe enough for print because they were selected manually from the aligned Bible corpus rather than copied wholesale from noisy autogenerated sections. Genesis 2:17 is especially strong because it contrasts `ne` and `nek` in the same verse and also contains `theihna`. Genesis 19:19 and 2 Samuel 23:8 are likewise useful because they show `muhna` and `neih` in clear derived environments.

# What does not yet work

The current report layer is not yet reliable enough to auto-generate this chapter. `output/grammar/grammar_full.md` and `output/grammar/example_selection_audit.md` still leave stem alternation as review-only, and several sections of `docs/grammar/reports/05-verb-11-vsa-questionnaire.md` remain visibly noisy. That means the project can support a print-facing slice, but not yet a hands-off pipeline for choosing stem-alternation examples.

The biggest remaining descriptive problem is environmental overstatement. The literature review supports a broad association of Form II with negatives, nominalizations, and certain derived constructions, but the current questionnaire summary does not line up cleanly enough to let the printed chapter collapse everything into one rigid rule. The present slice handles this correctly by making the nominalized and dependent evidence central and by keeping the negative distribution slightly hedged.

Some candidate verb pairs were also omitted on purpose. `za/zak`, `pua/puak`, and some automatically surfaced `pia/piak` material still need closer manual review before they are safe as routine print examples. The slice is better for being selective.

# Citation and source audit

The grammar slice cites only bibliography keys that already exist in `literature/bibliography.bib`: `@henderson1965` and `@zamngaihcing2017`. No new bibliography entry was needed.

Source use is appropriately layered. The literature review establishes the high-level Form I / Form II analysis; the generated reports identify likely stem pairs and flag current weak spots; and the aligned Bible corpus supplies the actual print examples. That separation is important, because the slice depends on the reports for discovery, but not on them as final authority for wording or example choice.

# Dictionary integration notes

Dictionary representation of stem alternation now looks viable. The paired-headword format works well for `mu ~ muh`, `ne ~ nek`, and `nei ~ neih`, where the relation is easy to explain and the examples are clean. It also works for less settled pairs such as `thei ~ theih`, `piang ~ pian`, and `pia ~ piak`, provided that the entry status line remains visible and the prose tells the reader where the evidence is strongest.

The key editorial principle is to keep the pair together while allowing uneven statuses. Not every verb needs to be called draft-ready at once. A mixed packet with some draft-ready entries and some clearly flagged review entries is better than either a false sense of closure or a refusal to print anything until every alternating verb is solved.

# Decision for next slice

This stem-alternation packet is good enough to stand as a third print-facing model for Tedim editorial work. It shows that a core verbal morphology topic can be assembled from literature, corpus evidence, and dictionary formatting without changing analyzer logic. The next slice can now move forward, but it should remain comparably narrow and manually curated rather than jumping immediately to a broad TAM chapter.
