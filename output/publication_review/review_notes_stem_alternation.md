# What works

This packet is now strong enough to show that verb stem alternation can be handled as a real print-facing topic rather than as a backend memo. The grammar slice no longer implies that only the cleanest three pairs deserve discussion, but it also no longer treats every analyzer pair as if it were a lexical verb. Instead, it now distinguishes lexical-pair status, Bible attestation profile, lexical category, and promotion status.

The literature and the corpus agree on the core point. Henderson and Zam Ngaih Cing clearly describe a two-form system, and the current corpus/paradigm outputs confirm that paired stems such as `mu/muh`, `ne/nek`, `nei/neih`, `thei/theih`, `piang/pian`, `pia/piak`, `za/zak`, and `nusia/nusiat` are genuinely present in Tedim. The strongest examples are still the ones that show Form I in finite predication and Form II in dependent or nominalized environments rather than relying on abstract labels alone.

The chosen examples are safe enough for print because they were selected manually from the aligned Bible corpus rather than copied wholesale from noisy autogenerated sections. Genesis 2:17 is especially strong because it contrasts `ne` and `nek` in the same verse and also contains `theihna`. Genesis 19:19 and 2 Samuel 23:8 are likewise useful because they show `muhna` and `neih` in clear derived environments, while Genesis 24:52 and Deuteronomy 2:14 now anchor the caveated `za/zak` and `nusia/nusiat` extensions.

The representative matrix, lexical inventory, and new promotable-examples file have improved the review process. The matrix should still be read as a review layer rather than as an automatic quotation bank, and the lexical inventory should be read as a broader descriptive layer rather than as a print-example whitelist. Promoted matrix rows are tied either to exact accepted candidate tokens or to a very small set of manually checked caveated rows, while the lexical inventory now makes explicit which rows are lexical verbs, which are controls, and which are blocked nominal or analyzer-only items.

# What does not yet work

The current report layer is not yet reliable enough to auto-generate this chapter. `output/grammar/grammar_full.md` and `output/grammar/example_selection_audit.md` still leave stem alternation as review-only, and several sections of `docs/grammar/reports/05-verb-11-vsa-questionnaire.md` remain visibly noisy. That means the project can support a print-facing slice and a broad lexical inventory, but not yet a hands-off pipeline for choosing stem-alternation examples.

The biggest remaining descriptive problem is environmental overstatement. The literature review supports a broad association of Form II with negatives, nominalizations, and certain derived constructions, but the current questionnaire summary does not line up cleanly enough to let the printed chapter collapse everything into one rigid rule. The present slice handles this correctly by making the nominalized and dependent evidence central and by keeping the negative distribution slightly hedged.

Some candidate verb pairs are still difficult on purpose. `ngai/ngaih` remains dossier-only because of the `ngaihsun/ngaihsut` lexical-family problem, `thei/theih` and `piang/pian` remain provisional because their best Bible support is heavily constructional, `pua/puak` still needs closer manual review, and `honkhia/honkhiat` plus `hu/huh` remain excluded from simple stem-alternation treatment. At the same time, obviously nominal analyzer rows such as `mual/mualh`, `sum/sumh`, and `thu/thuh` are now kept out of the promoted verbal inventory rather than being allowed to inflate the grammar table.

# Candidate-layer note

Stem alternation now also has an analyzer-aware candidate file at `output/publication_review/candidates_stem_alternation.tsv`. That file records accepted core pair evidence, including the packet's central `mu ~ muh`, `ne ~ nek`, and `nei ~ neih` rows plus manually controlled `pia ~ piak`, `za ~ zak`, and `nusia ~ nusiat` evidence, while also preserving excluded or cautionary rows for questionnaire/report noise such as `piangsak`, `ngaihsutna`, and solitary `honkhiat`.

The packet should now be read in the order `candidate file -> matrix -> lexical inventory -> dossier -> grammar slice -> dictionary slice -> review notes`. The candidate layer does not mean every report/questionnaire pair is print-safe. It means the packet now has an explicit analyzer-aware record of which rows survive manual review, while the lexical inventory keeps broader one-sided and constructionally complex items visible without over-promoting them.

# Citation and source audit

The grammar slice cites only bibliography keys that already exist in `literature/bibliography.bib`: `@henderson1965` and `@zamngaihcing2017`. No new bibliography entry was needed.

Source use is appropriately layered. The literature review establishes the high-level Form I / Form II analysis; the generated reports identify likely stem pairs and flag current weak spots; the lexical inventory separates source status from Bible attestation and print safety; and the aligned Bible corpus supplies the actual print examples. That separation is important, because the slice depends on the reports for discovery, but not on them as final authority for wording or example choice.

# Dictionary integration notes

Dictionary representation of stem alternation now looks viable. The paired-headword format works well for `mu ~ muh`, `ne ~ nek`, and `nei ~ neih`, where the relation is easy to explain and the examples are clean. It now also works for `za ~ zak` as a print-usable entry with caveat and for `nusia ~ nusiat` as a next-stage caveated entry, because both pairs now have manually checked examples and visible status labels.

Less settled pairs such as `thei ~ theih` and `piang ~ pian` remain in the slice with explicit caution rather than being forced into draft-ready status. `pia ~ piak` has improved enough to stay in the slice as print-usable with caveat, while `ngai ~ ngaih`, `honkhia ~ honkhiat`, and `hu ~ huh` remain outside the dictionary slice for now because the lexical-family, derivational, or category-mixing problems are still too large.

The key editorial principle is to keep the pair together while allowing uneven statuses. Not every verb needs to be called draft-ready at once. A mixed packet with some draft-ready entries and some clearly flagged review entries is better than either a false sense of closure or a refusal to print anything until every alternating verb is solved.

# Decision for next slice

The dossier has now been reviewed and incorporated into the expanded print slice and lexical inventory. The grammar slice includes a larger lexical table; `za ~ zak`, `pia ~ piak`, and `nusia ~ nusiat` are treated consistently as print-usable with caveat; same-form questionnaire items are retained as one-sided Bible attestations rather than suppressed; and `ngai ~ ngaih`, `honkhia ~ honkhiat`, and `hu ~ huh` remain dossier-only or excluded for now.

The stem-alternation packet is now closer to serving as a solid third print-facing model, but the next step is still to keep reviewing and tightening this packet rather than to move on to case marking immediately. It now shows that a core verbal morphology topic can be assembled from literature, corpus evidence, a hardened review matrix, a broader lexical inventory, and dictionary formatting without changing analyzer logic, while still keeping derivational filtering and manual example review explicit.
